Everyone has a different rhythm. Mine is five breaths a minute.

Six a minute is the number in every breathing app. It is an average, and averages belong to nobody. Below is a week of my own heartbeat — at my desk, in traffic, in meetings, sitting still — and the moments where what I felt and what a wearable would have told me point in opposite directions.

One · find the number

The sweet spot is personal, and it is measurable in ten minutes

Two minutes at each pace. The bars are how far my heart rate travelled inside a single breath — the size of the vagal brake being applied. The peak is mine; yours will sit somewhere else.

What the curve says

My response peaks between 5 and 5.5 breaths a minute and falls away on both sides. At 4.5 — slower, which sounds better — it drops by a third. Slower is not automatically deeper.

Why it matters

Every guided-breathing feature ships one default. Practising at someone else's number means working harder for a smaller effect, and concluding that breathwork does not do much for you.

Left alone

Unpaced afterwards I settled at about 4 breaths a minute, with a larger swing than any guided rate produced. The instrument found something the protocol could not ask for.

    Three · the arena changes the meaning

    A brake you apply sitting still is not the brake you need at 11am

    Three settings, three different physiologies. Most tools were validated in the third and are sold for the first two.

    Arena 1 · the desk

    Office, unscripted

    79 min · 5,532 beats · 0.1% noise

    Talking and switching tasks scattered my heart — variability rose every time. Dropping into focused work with music on took 7 beats off and halved the variability for fifteen minutes.

    The catch: my best focus and my worst discomfort produce the same signature. Going quiet is not one state, and no heartbeat alone tells them apart.

    Arena 2 · the cushion

    Sitting practice

    5 sits · 2.5–4 breaths/min reached

    The breath slows and deepens, then begins to pause. Twice the heart's swing flattened for 15–25 seconds — the signature of a held breath — and I wrote down what had happened half a minute later.

    The catch: the deeper it goes, the worse it scores. A light breath moves the heart less, so every variability number falls exactly when the practice is working.

    Arena 3 · the treadmill

    Load and recovery

    90 min volleyball vs 20 min yoga

    Volleyball left me 17 beats above baseline for hours. Twenty minutes of yoga left me below my own resting rate, reporting "light and energised".

    The catch: both are logged as exertion. One strain number averages two opposite effects.

    What this is for

    Evidence a coach and a client can look at together

    A professional coaching conversation runs on the client's own awareness. The usual difficulty is that awareness arrives late — you notice you were tense after the meeting, not during it.

    This is a way to put a second account on the table. Not a score and not a verdict: a trace of what the body did, beside what the person said at the time, so the two can be compared where they disagree.

    • The client keeps the interpretation. The data does not overrule what they felt — twice here it was the wearer's own words that corrected the reading, not the other way round.
    • It is specific to one person. A sweet spot of five, a resting breath of six a minute: those are mine, and the method finds yours rather than handing you the average.
    • It turns practice into something with a visible target — a rate to breathe at, and a way to see whether it carried into the hour afterwards.
    What it is not

    Where I would stop short

    • One person, one week. Everything here is n=1. Three other people have started; nothing is replicated yet.
    • No medical claim. This is wellbeing and self-awareness. It diagnoses nothing and treats nothing.
    • Breathing rate is inferred from the heart, not measured at the chest. Where two independent methods disagree, the page shows nothing rather than a confident number — that happens in about four blocks in ten.
    • The alerting rule does not work yet. It fires on focused work as readily as on discomfort, so it stays switched off until it can ask rather than assume.
